# Task Slicing

Big tasks are ambushes. Slices are snacks.

## Why "clean your room" never gets done

Try this experiment right now. Ask your brain to "clean your room." Feel that
sinking, scrolling-away, might-as-well-lie-down feeling? That's not laziness.
That's your brain refusing an invoice with no itemization.

"Clean your room" quietly bundles dozens of decisions: where to start, what
counts as clean, where does this shirt go, should I keep this box, how long
will this take, what if I run out of time halfway and it's worse than before.
For a low-energy brain, that bundle costs more than the whole task.

**Task slicing** means doing the itemization on paper first — *at full energy,
in advance* — so that on execution day, there are no decisions left, only
motion. Decision-making is where low-energy days bleed out. Slicing pre-pays
the decision tax.

## The rules of a good slice

A slice is a good slice when:

1. **It takes 2–10 minutes of motion.** Not "as long as it takes."
2. **It has a visible finish.** You can point at the result. "Dishes into the
   dishwasher" — yes. "Deal with kitchen" — no.
3. **It requires no decisions.** Every choice was made when you wrote the
   slice.
4. **It's grammatically small.** One verb, one object. "Wipe oven door," not
   "clean kitchen part 3."

If a slice needs a decision, it's actually a *plan*, not a task. Untangle it
while you're still planning.

## How to slice a chore (worked example)

Chore: **the kitchen is wrecked.** Do this on paper, ideally before the kitchen
day arrives:

1. Trash into the bin (3 min)
2. Dishes into dishwasher, start it (5 min)
3. Hand-wash the two things that don't fit (4 min)
4. Back into fridge: the three containers (3 min)
5. Wipe counters (3 min)
6. Sweep floor (5 min)

Then the slicing trick that changes everything: **the "first slice only" rule.**

On the actual day, you only commit to slice 1: trash into the bin. You are
allowed to stop after that, with our formal blessing. Completing slice 1 and
stopping is a *success*, recorded in your chore planner as done.

What usually happens instead: slice 1 creates a tiny bit of momentum, 40% of
the time you roll into slice 2 unprompted, and some days the kitchen gets
clean "by accident." The 60% of days you stop after slice 1? The kitchen is
still 5% cleaner, and your trust in yourself grew by one data point. Trust
compounds.

## Slicing for non-chores

The same rules work for paperwork, emails, phone calls, and study sessions:

- "Do taxes" → slice 1: "Find the W-2 and put it in the red folder." That's it.
  That is an entire, legitimate task.
- "Clean out email" → slice 1: "Delete everything older than one year from the
  inbox, don't read any of it, just old."
- "Study chapter 4" → slice 1: "Read the headings and the summary box. Nothing
  else."

## The 2-minute versions

Every chore planner entry deserves a *minimum viable version* — the version you
would still do at 15% battery. Write it next to the real slice:

| Real slice | 2-minute version |
|------------|------------------|
| Wipe all counters | Wipe the stove-side counter only |
| Full trash roundup | Empty the bathroom trash |
| Prune inbox to zero | Delete 10 old emails |
| Write chapter draft | Write two sentences |

Low-battery days exist so the 2-minute versions get their turn. They keep the
streak alive, and the streak is what keeps the system from dying in a bad week
(see [Restart Rituals](06-restart-rituals.md)).
