Energy Accounting

Energy is a budget. Money skills translate almost perfectly.

The core idea

You already know money: you earn it, you spend it, and if you spend more than you earn, you go into debt. Energy works the same way, with one cruel twist — you can't take out a loan against tomorrow.

  • Earning energy: sleep, food, water, movement, sunlight, genuine rest.
  • Spending energy: chores, work, decisions, socializing, even fun things.
  • Overdraft: short naps that don't count, "I'll rest tonight" promissory notes, caffeine advances.

Most people with low energy aren't lazy. They're just spending from a smaller account. The fix isn't to earn more first (though sleep helps) — it's to stop budgeting like the account is bigger than it is.

Step 1: Rate your account

Each morning — or whenever you actually get moving — ask one question:

"What percentage battery do I have right now?"

Pick a gut number, not a fair number. 20% is allowed. 15% is allowed. Some days you wake up at 40% after a full night's sleep. That's data, not failure.

BatteryWhat it meansToday's mode
70–100%Rare, precious dayDo the big scary thing
40–69%A normal low-energy daySlices + one anchor task
15–39%Running on fumesMaintenance only: dishes, meds, one load of laundry
Below 15%Crash territorySurvival protocol: rest, snacks, no timers

The full worksheet for this is in Worksheets: Energy Ledger.

Step 2: Learn your costs

Every task has an energy price that is personal to you. A chore planner makes chores look equal because they take equal minutes. They don't take equal energy.

Example: two people, two chores.

TaskPerson APerson B
20 min of dishes3 units8 units
Sorting mail for 10 min1 unit6 units

Person B could do dishes for an hour if you paid them. Mail, though, is a dungeon. Neither person is wrong — the prices are just different.

To find your prices, keep a tiny ledger for one week. After each task, jot a number from 1–10 for how much it cost. Don't analyze; guess. Guessing is a measurement skill and it improves fast.

Clues for reading your ledger:

  • Tasks you avoid for weeks are usually high-cost — avoidance is your accounting software speaking.
  • Tasks with a "setup toll" (getting cleaning supplies out, changing clothes) cost more than their minutes suggest.
  • Ambiguous tasks cost extra. "Clean the kitchen" is expensive; "run the dishwasher and wipe the counters" is cheap, because the price is knowable in advance.

Step 3: Spend on purpose

Once you know roughly what today has and what things cost, you can spend deliberately:

  1. Pick today's anchor task — the one thing that would make the day a win. On a 50% day, the anchor should cost about a quarter of your battery, not half of it.
  2. Add one to three slices (see Task Slicing).
  3. Stop scheduling when the imaginary total hits ~80% of your battery. People chronically over-budget by about 20%. Leave the margin in.

That's it. That's the whole method: know the balance, know the prices, spend on purpose, keep a margin.

When the ledger says "overdrawn three days in a row"

Three overdrawn days in a row isn't a discipline problem. It's a signal that your regular week costs more than your regular income, which means the routine itself needs re-pricing — fewer units per day, cheaper tasks, or more deliberate rest (coming up in Guilt-Free Rest). Whichever you choose, the ledger will show you whether it worked. Let the data argue with your inner critic; data usually wins.
