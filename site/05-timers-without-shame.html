Timers Without Shame

Timers are the most-recommended and most-abandoned ADHD tool. Here's how to keep them.

Why timers go wrong

The classic sequence: you set a 25-minute timer, work for 25 minutes, the timer goes off, you feel ... interrupted at best. Or: you set the timer, the timer becomes a ticker of your own inadequacy ("39 minutes left and I've done NOTHING"), and now the timer is emotionally attached to each minute of underperformance — a guilt metronome.

The problem was never the timer. It was the verdict: timers were framing each ring as a judgment about how much you produced. We fix the frame, then the timer becomes neutral again — just a windshield, not a scoreboard.

Rule 1: The timer measures time, not you

Write this where you'll see it:

The timer is a clock doing one job. Its only output is a sound. It is not a grade.

A timer that ends and finds nothing done has done its job perfectly: it measured time, and time passed. Time passing is not failure; it's the raw material you were going to spend anyway.

Rule 2: Timers mean "check in," not "stop"

The 25-minute pomodoro default quietly assumes you'll stop and take a break — which lands as either an interruption (you're in flow!) or a demerit (you didn't finish!). Reframe: the ring is a check-in question, not an order.

When it rings, ask one thing: "In, park, or pivot?"

  • In: it's working, keep rolling. Set the next check-in further out — 45–60 minutes — because you're in flow and flow isn't for interrupting.
  • Park: the task beat you for now. You park it with a breadcrumb, not as a failure (see below). Then pick a rest from Guilt-Free Rest.
  • Pivot: you've drifted onto a different task. Decide on purpose whether to keep it.

Two of three options are not "you did badly." That ratio is the design.

Rule 3: The Park Breadcrumb rule

Never leave a paused task without one line that future-you can pick up:

Parked: kitchen counter — dishes are IN the dishwasher, the containers were the blockers, start at the containers.

Future-you at 40% battery is a different person with less knowledge. The breadcrumb is a note to them — kind, terse, no commentary. Same mechanic as the restart ritual in Restart Rituals; you're building the habit in the small crashes first.

Rule 4: Different timers for different jobs

TimerLengthJob
Slice timer5–10 minOne slice from Task Slicing
Check-in timer25–45 minWork block, no verdict attached
Rest timer20–40 minContainer for Guilt-Free Rest
Transition timer2–5 minThe gap between doing and resting (or resting and doing)
Overdue alarmone-timeThe ONE thing with an actual deadline (meds, pickup)

The transition timer is the secret best one. 2 minutes of "standing up, closing tabs, walking to the couch" — ends the session with a physical action instead of the timer snapping the day off like a branch.

Rule 5: Apps that don't punish

Pick timer apps that:

  • Ring and stop. No streak counters you can lose. No "days since you missed."
  • Are visible but calm: big font, one number, dark screen at night.
  • Have a guilt-free restart. If abandoning it mid-session costs guilt, it's the wrong app.

Plain kitchen timers are allowed. Phone timers are allowed. If a timer has ever shamed you, it lost its right to be your timer — that's not you failing, that's a design bug.

The one legit shame-timer exception

The only place timers with teeth help: deadlines that protect other people — grandmother's ride, the pharmacy pickup, putting dinner in the oven. Even then, set TWO alarms: one at "deal with it now" and one at "point of no return." The first is the fence at the top of the cliff; the second is the ambulance at the bottom.
